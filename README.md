# Recognition Agreements Across Borders and Sectors

Structured methods for mutual recognition of credentials.

Course ID: ICB-A11 | ICSF skill ECOS "Ecosystem and recognition partnerships" | Level L4 "Assure" | Stage A "Ability"

## Recognition models

### Why recognition agreements exist

A credential has value when someone other than the issuer trusts it. A recognition agreement is a written understanding between two or more parties about how they will treat each other's credentials. It tells earners what they can expect, tells verifiers what they can rely on, and tells issuers what they must maintain.

Recognition can take several forms. A professional body may accept a university's programme as meeting part of its entry requirements. An employer may agree to treat a sector association's micro-credential as evidence for a promotion step. Two national agencies may agree that credentials at a stated level in one framework can be compared with credentials at a stated level in another.

Without an agreement, each recognition decision is made case by case. That is slow, inconsistent and hard to explain to earners. A good agreement makes decisions repeatable and open to review.

In this course, "recognition" means a documented decision to give a credential a defined status or effect in another organisation's context. The effect might be admission, exemption from a part of a programme, credit, licence to practise, hiring preference or access to a role. You must always state the effect, because "we recognise each other's credentials" is too vague to apply.

### Four recognition models

Most agreements fit one of four models. Real agreements often combine them.

**Unilateral recognition.** One party decides to accept another party's credentials. The other party does not need to agree or reciprocate. A professional body that lists approved programmes works this way.

**Bilateral mutual recognition.** Two parties accept each other's credentials on stated terms. Both carry obligations, so you need clear terms on quality and evidence.

**Multilateral agreement.** Several parties sign one framework agreement and apply the same terms to all. This reduces repeat negotiation but needs stronger governance, because one weak partner affects everyone.

**Referencing to a shared framework.** Parties do not compare credentials directly. Each maps its credentials to a common reference, such as a qualifications framework level or a skills taxonomy, and recognition follows from the mapping.

Choose the model by asking three questions. How many parties will take part? How much do they trust each other's quality assurance already? How often will credentials change? Few parties with high trust suit a bilateral agreement. Many parties with varied quality assurance suit referencing to a shared framework with a clear review cycle.

The body of the agreement states the principles and obligations. The annexes hold the detail that changes more often, such as quality criteria and data formats. Keeping detail in annexes means you can update it without reopening the whole agreement.

### Scope and effect clauses

The first clause you write is scope. State which credentials are covered, which are excluded, and what effect recognition has. A useful scope clause answers five questions:

1. Which issuers and which credentials are covered?
2. Which receiving parties will recognise them?
3. What effect does recognition have (for example credit, exemption, hiring criterion)?
4. Is recognition automatic or subject to a further check?
5. How long does it last?

Avoid the word "equivalent" unless you define it. Equivalence can mean same level, same content, same outcome or same legal status, and parties often mean different things.

**Worked example: Association and public agency agree on a micro-credential**

A national sector association for building services engineers (a lime party) issues a short micro-credential on energy audit practice. A regional public agency (also lime, as policy) funds training for energy advisers and wants to accept the micro-credential as one of the entry requirements for its adviser register.

The programme lead at the association and the registrar at the agency work through the scope questions. They choose unilateral recognition first, because the agency has no credentials of its own to offer in return. The scope clause reads, in plain terms: "The agency accepts the association's Energy Audit Practice micro-credential as meeting entry requirement 3 of the adviser register, provided the credential was issued after the date of the first approved assessment cycle and is verified through the association's published verification service."

They exclude older certificates issued before the assessment was redesigned. They set the effect as "meets one entry requirement", not "grants a place on the register". They set the term at three years, with a review before renewal.

A second context shows the same method in a company. A corporate academy at a logistics group (an orange party) has an internal safety leadership badge. The HR department wants to recognise a sector association's external safety certificate as a substitute for the first module of the internal pathway. They choose a bilateral approach in the long run, because the academy's badge may later be recognised by the association's members. For now they sign a unilateral clause with a review point after twelve months.

All numbers and details in these examples are illustrative.

## Quality expectations

### From trust to criteria

Parties recognise credentials when they trust the way the credentials were awarded. Trust becomes workable when it is written as criteria that another party can check. Quality expectations are those criteria. They describe what must be true about a credential, its issuer and its assessment before the receiving party accepts it.

Differing quality expectations are the main source of friction in negotiations. An association may stress peer-reviewed assessment. An employer may care more about recent, workplace-based evidence. A public agency may need documented quality assurance because public money is involved. None of these views is wrong. Your task as negotiator is to find which expectations matter for the effect you want, and agree how to check them.

### Four areas of quality expectation

Most quality criteria fall into four areas. Use them as a checklist when you build an annex.

**Issuer quality.** Who issues the credential and how are they governed? Typical criteria: legal status, accreditation or approval by a recognised body, published quality assurance, complaints procedure.

**Learning outcomes and level.** What does the credential claim the earner can do, and at what level? Typical criteria: outcomes stated in clear terms, a stated level in a named framework, a workload or volume statement.

**Assessment and evidence.** How was achievement judged? Typical criteria: assessment methods, assessor competence, identity checks, authenticity controls, grading rules, handling of appeals.

**Credential integrity.** Can the receiving party trust the credential itself? Typical criteria: a verifiable digital format, issue and expiry dates, revocation handling, data protection, a link to the issuer's published information.

### Writing criteria that a partner can check

A criterion is useful when a person who has never met the issuer can decide whether it is met. Test each criterion with three questions. Is it observable? Is the evidence named? Is the threshold clear?

Compare two versions:

- Weak: "The issuer has good quality assurance."
- Stronger: "The issuer publishes a quality assurance policy that covers assessment, appeals and review, and has been externally reviewed in the last five years. Evidence: link to the policy and the review statement."

Write criteria as short statements with an evidence line. Give each a number so that later reviews and disputes can refer to it.

### Exchanging evidence

Quality criteria need an evidence route. Decide what evidence travels between parties, how, and how often. Options include:

- **Document exchange at the start.** The issuer sends its quality assurance policy, assessment specification and sample credential once, before the agreement takes effect.
- **Published information.** The issuer keeps a public page with current criteria and credential details, and the receiving party checks it when needed.
- **Periodic reports.** The issuer sends a short annual report on volumes, appeals, changes and incidents.
- **Per-credential verification.** The receiving party checks each credential through a verification service.
- **Sampling and audit.** The receiving party, or an agreed third party, reviews a sample of assessments.

Choose the lightest route that gives the receiving party enough confidence for the effect they grant. A hiring preference needs less evidence than a licence to practise. Agree also what each party does with the evidence, including how long it is kept and who may see it. Where evidence contains personal data, state the legal basis and protection measures in the agreement, and involve your data protection lead.

**Worked example: Agency and provider agree on an evidence route**

A public agency responsible for adult learning (lime) wants to fund learners who hold a digital skills micro-credential from a private training provider (cyan). The agency's programme lead and the provider's quality manager draft the annex.

The agency's concern is public money. It needs to know that assessment is independent and that credentials cannot be forged. The provider's concern is workload. It does not want to send large files every month.

They agree the following criteria and routes, shown in simplified form:

- Criterion Q1: assessors are independent of the teaching team. Evidence: assessor policy, supplied at the start and after any change.
- Criterion Q2: each credential states outcomes, level and date. Evidence: sample credential, supplied at the start.
- Criterion Q3: credentials can be verified online. Evidence: the agency checks each credential through the provider's verification service.
- Criterion Q4: appeals are handled within a stated period. Evidence: annual report with the number of appeals and outcomes.

In a second context, an HR director at a manufacturing company (orange) and a sector association (lime) agree that the company will treat the association's welding micro-credential as proof of a practical test. The company asks for sampling: once a year, its own trainer observes three assessments. The association accepts, because it lets the company see the standard first-hand without extra paperwork each time.

Figures in this example are illustrative.

## Cross-border issues

### Three kinds of difference

When parties sit in different countries, three kinds of difference appear in almost every negotiation: legal, language and level. You do not need to resolve them all at once. You need to name them, decide who is responsible for each, and write clauses that survive disagreement.

**Legal differences.** Countries regulate education, professions, data protection and contracts in different ways. A credential may have formal legal effect in one country and none in another. A profession may be regulated in one country and open in another. Your agreement must say which law applies to the agreement itself, which law applies to credentials and professions, and what happens when they conflict. Obtain advice from qualified legal counsel for any clause with legal effect. This course teaches you to frame the questions, not to give legal advice.

**Language differences.** Credentials may be issued in one language but read in another. Terms such as "diploma", "certificate" and "licence" do not translate cleanly. The agreement must say which language version is authoritative, who translates, and how translations of credentials and criteria are checked.

**Level differences.** Qualification systems describe levels in different ways. Countries may use national frameworks with different numbers of levels and different descriptors. Micro-credentials may sit outside a national framework entirely. Level comparisons need a documented method and a statement of what the comparison does and does not mean.

### Reference points you can use

Several shared reference points help parties compare credentials. This course names them without going into every detail, and you should check current texts before relying on them.

- The **European Qualifications Framework (EQF)** is an eight-level reference that national frameworks refer to. Many European countries have referenced their frameworks to it.
- The **Council Recommendation on a European approach to micro-credentials for lifelong learning and employability (2022)** sets out a common description of a micro-credential and standard elements for describing one, along with principles for quality, transparency, recognition and portability. Its status is **Established**: it is a stable reference. Watch for Commission implementation reporting and national transpositions, which differ between Member States. <!-- VERIFY: current state of national transpositions and Commission implementation reports before use in live sessions. -->
- The **EUDI Wallet under eIDAS 2.0** is an **Emerging** topic. Member States must offer at least one wallet by 24 December 2026. Germany plans its state wallet ("d-you") for 2 January 2027, with basic functions first. Regulated private sectors must accept the wallet about one year after issuance starts. Readiness varies widely between Member States. For agreements, these reference points offer vocabulary and a starting method. They do not remove the need to check the facts for each partner country.

### Clauses that handle differences

Write cross-border clauses that do four things.

1. **Name the governing law and forum** for the agreement itself, after advice from legal counsel. If parties cannot agree, state that the agreement is a non-binding memorandum of understanding and say so clearly.
2. **Separate the agreement from legal effects.** State that the agreement does not change the legal rules on regulated professions or on formal recognition procedures in any country. Where a legal procedure exists, the agreement can speed up the evidence step but cannot replace the decision of the competent authority.
3. **Set language rules.** State the authoritative language version, who produces translations, and that credential titles stay in the original language with a translation alongside.
4. **Set a level method.** State which reference is used, who does the comparison, how disputes about level are handled, and that level comparison supports the recognition decision but is not the only factor.

Data protection deserves its own clause. State which personal data is exchanged, the legal basis, the roles of each party, retention periods and the route for earner requests. Involve your data protection officer early.

**Worked example: Three partners compare levels and languages**

A professional body for accountants in one country (lime) wants to recognise a short finance micro-credential from a college in a neighbouring country (cyan) as partial evidence for its continuing development scheme. A multinational's corporate academy (orange) also wants to use the same credential for its regional finance team.

The programme lead builds the comparison grid. The professional body's country regulates the profession. The college's country does not. The college's credential is issued in the local language only. Its level is stated on a national framework that the college's country has referenced to the EQF, but the professional body uses a different internal scale. <!-- VERIFY: any real referencing claims before turning this into a live case. -->

They agree these clauses:

- The agreement is a memorandum of understanding between the professional body and the college. It does not alter any rule on who may practise the profession.
- The professional body accepts the credential for continuing development points only. It does not accept it for entry to the profession.
- The authoritative language is English. The college provides a certified English summary of outcomes, and the credential title stays in the original language.
- Level is compared through the shared EQF reference, with a short note from the college on its national framework level. The professional body's panel reviews the note and may ask for more evidence.

The corporate academy signs a separate, lighter letter of agreement with the college. It accepts the credential as evidence for an internal learning path, with no legal effect beyond the company.

## Governance

### Why agreements need governance

An agreement without governance decays. People change roles, assessment methods change, credentials are updated and complaints arrive. Governance is the set of roles, routines and rules that keep an agreement accurate and trusted over time. For a recognition agreement it answers four questions: who decides, how do we monitor, how do we change things, and how do we end it.

Good governance is proportionate. A light letter of agreement between two organisations needs a named contact on each side and an annual check. A multilateral framework with a dozen issuers needs a steering group, a secretariat and an admission process. Choose the smallest structure that lets you keep the promises in the agreement.

### Core governance elements

**Roles.** Name an owner for the agreement in each organisation. State who can speak for the organisation, who receives notices and who is the contact for earners and verifiers. For multilateral agreements, set up a steering group with defined membership and decision rules, such as consensus or majority voting.

**Monitoring.** State what is monitored, by whom and how often. Typical items are the quality criteria in the annex, changes to credentials, complaints, verification failures, and use of the agreement (for example the number of recognition decisions made). Keep monitoring light but regular.

**Change control.** Set how the agreement and its annexes can be changed. The body may need all parties to agree. Annexes may be updated by the steering group with notice. Record each version with a date and a summary of changes. Say how long before a change takes effect, and what happens to credentials issued under earlier versions.

**Review.** Set review dates and triggers. Fixed reviews might be annual for operational matters and every three years for the agreement as a whole. Triggers might include a change in law, a major quality incident, a change of issuer ownership or a new framework. Describe what the review looks at and what outcomes are possible: continue, amend, suspend or end.

**Disputes and complaints.** Provide an escalation path. First contact between named owners, then a senior review, then a mediator or other agreed route. Provide a complaints route for earners and verifiers, and say how earners are protected while a dispute is open.

**Suspension and exit.** State when a party's recognition can be suspended, for example after a serious breach of a quality criterion, and how earners holding credentials are treated. State how a party can leave, the notice period and the fate of already recognised credentials. Protecting earners is a core part of trustworthiness: credentials recognised in good faith should usually keep their effect for a stated period.

### Protecting trust and value

Two ideas from the VRITE pillars guide governance. **Value** asks whether the agreement still benefits earners, issuers and receiving parties. Track whether recognition decisions are being made, whether earners are using the effect and whether partners still find the effort worthwhile. If nobody uses an agreement, review it or end it. **Trustworthiness** asks whether partners and earners can rely on what the agreement says. Publish a short summary of the agreement, the quality criteria and the current partners. Make complaint routes visible. Record decisions and changes with dates.

Agreements often use AI tools for drafting, translation or screening of evidence. General rules apply. Keep a person accountable for each decision. Do not send personal data or confidential material to tools without approval. Check machine translations of legal and quality terms with a qualified person. Record in the governance clauses where automated tools are used to process evidence, and keep a route for human review of decisions that affect an earner.

**Worked example: Governance for a sector agreement and a corporate agreement**

A sector association for hospitality (lime) signs a bilateral agreement with a public agency (lime) that runs a national skills register. The agreement lets the agency list the association's front-of-house micro-credential as proof of one skill. The two named owners are the association's education manager and the agency's register manager. They meet once a year to check the annex. They agree that the annex may change with ninety days' notice if both owners agree, and that credentials issued under earlier versions remain valid for two years. A review of the whole agreement is set for the third year, with an earlier review if the agency changes the register's rules. Complaints from earners go first to the issuer, then to a joint panel of both owners. Figures are illustrative.

A corporate academy (orange) at an energy company agrees with two universities (cyan) to recognise each other's data skills badges inside a small consortium. Because three parties take part, they set up a steering group with one seat each and decisions by consensus. The academy lead acts as secretary and keeps a public change log. If a university changes assessment methods, it must notify the group before the change takes effect, and the group decides whether recognition continues. An exit clause gives six months' notice and protects badges already recognised. The academy HR department adds a clause that any automated screening of evidence is checked by a named person before a decision reaches an earner.

[(X)] The credentials covered, the receiving parties and the effect of recognition
[( )] The marketing plan for the credentials
[( )] The salary of each earner
[[?]] Scope says what is covered and what recognition does.
****
A scope clause names the credentials, the parties and the effect of recognition, together with conditions and duration. Names of earners, marketing plans and salaries do not belong in it.
****

**Question 3.** Which criterion is written so that a partner can check it?

[( )] Assessment is robust.
[( )] The issuer is reputable.
[(X)] Assessors are independent of the teaching team; evidence: assessor policy supplied at the start and after any change.
[( )] The credential is high quality.
[[?]] Look for a named item of evidence.
****
The third criterion states an observable condition and names the evidence. The others rely on words such as "robust", "reputable" and "high quality" without any way to check them.
****

**Question 4.** An employer grants only a hiring preference based on a partner's credential. Which evidence route is proportionate?

[( )] An annual audit of every assessment by the employer
[(X)] Published issuer information and verification of each credential
[( )] A joint accreditation panel visiting every site
[( )] No evidence, because hiring preferences are informal
[[?]] Match the evidence to the weight of the effect.
****
A hiring preference has limited consequences, so published information and per-credential verification provide enough confidence. Audits and panels are disproportionate, and no evidence leaves the employer exposed.
****

**Question 5.** The issuer cannot share assessment papers for confidentiality reasons. What is the best response?

[( )] Remove the criterion on assessment
[( )] Insist on papers, because confidentiality is not a valid reason
[(X)] Keep the purpose of the criterion and agree another route, such as an assessment specification and an observation by an agreed third party
[( )] Postpone the decision to the first review
[[?]] Keep the purpose, change the route.
****
The purpose of the criterion is confidence in assessment. An alternative route that protects confidentiality still delivers that confidence. Removing or postponing the criterion weakens the agreement.
****

**Question 6.** What should an agreement say about a profession that is regulated in one partner's country?

[( )] The agreement overrides the regulator for partner credentials
[(X)] The agreement does not change the legal rules, and the competent authority still decides
[( )] The agreement applies only if the regulator signs it
[( )] Nothing, because regulation is outside the agreement
[[?]] An agreement between organisations cannot change the law.
****
Stating that the agreement does not alter legal rules protects the parties and earners and avoids false expectations. The competent authority keeps its decision.
****

**Question 7.** Which statement about the EUDI Wallet is correct for use in this course?

[( )] It is Established, and all partner countries have a working wallet today
[( )] It replaces the EQF
[(X)] It is Emerging, with Member State offerings required by 24 December 2026 and readiness varying widely, so you check each partner country
[( )] It is the same as the Council Recommendation on micro-credentials
[[?]] Look for the status label and the note on readiness.
****
The wallet is an emerging topic. Member States must offer at least one wallet by 24 December 2026, and readiness varies. Check each partner country and do not assume the wallet supports educational credentials. It is a separate matter from the EQF and the Recommendation.
****

**Question 8.** Two partners use different national level scales. What does the agreement do?

[( )] Treats the highest levels as equal
[( )] Avoids any level comparison
[(X)] States a documented method through a shared reference, who compares, how disputes are handled, and that level is one factor among others
[( )] Leaves level to each party to decide without explanation
[[?]] Document the method and its limits.
****
A documented method with a named responsible party and a dispute route is transparent and does not over-claim. Assuming equality or avoiding comparison weakens trust.
****

**Question 9.** Which review clause is the strongest?

[( )] The agreement will be reviewed when needed.
[(X)] The agreement is reviewed every three years, and earlier after a change in law or a major quality incident, with outcomes of continue, amend, suspend or end.
[( )] The owners will meet when they have time.
[( )] Review is optional.
[[?]] Fixed dates, named triggers and possible outcomes.
****
The strongest clause combines a fixed interval, defined triggers and a list of outcomes. The others leave timing and decisions open.
****

**Question 10.** An issuer exits an agreement. What should the exit clause do for earners?

[( )] Cancel all recognition immediately
[( )] Say nothing, since earners are not parties
[(X)] Keep recognition for credentials issued before exit for a stated period and set out how earners are informed
[( )] Require earners to retake the assessment
[[?]] Earners relied on the agreement in good faith.
****
Protecting earners who relied on the agreement is part of trustworthiness. A stated period and clear communication give them fair treatment. Immediate cancellation or silence harms them.
****
